About the desk

SymbiQ is written and maintained by Chinmoy. It is one person and a lot of checking, not an institution, and it has no funding from and no relationship with any company whose claims appear on it.

That matters because of what this site spends most of its time doing: telling you that a number you read somewhere is wrong, or right, or right about something other than what the headline said. A site that does that anonymously is asking you for trust it has not earned. So this page is the answer to “why should I believe any of this?”, and the answer is deliberately not “because of who wrote it.” It is because you can check it.

How a number gets onto this site

Every figure that carries weight here comes from a primary source — the paper, the standard, the press release, the regulation — not from somebody else’s summary of it. Where a number is worked out rather than quoted, the working is done in code that lives in the open, and it runs again on every build. Where a figure came out of a calculation you could reasonably want to see, the pages themselves show it: the receipt opens inline, next to the claim.

Several things run automatically and refuse to let the site ship if they disagree with it:

  • The arithmetic is re-derived, not trusted. Widgets, thresholds, game pars and resource estimates are checked by scripts that recompute them from scratch — often by exhaustive search over every possible input rather than by spot-checking a few. A game par on this site is a proven optimum or it does not ship.
  • Citations are checked for rot. Every arXiv identifier the site cites is asked, against arXiv itself, whether the paper still exists, whether it has been withdrawn, whether it has moved several versions ahead of the figure quoted, and whether the surrounding text actually names its authors or title. This exists because the worst error this site has made was a stale figure from a superseded paper.
  • Prose is checked against data. The hardest bugs here have never been arithmetic. They have been a sentence asserting a number that the machine-checked data three inches away contradicted. Guards now read the sentences too.

Claims are judged by rules written before the answer is known

The Ledger tracks public, dated, falsifiable claims about quantum computing from labs, executives and regulators. The rules it runs on are the point:

  • The resolution criteria and the exact sources that will be consulted are frozen when the claim is logged, long before the deadline. Changing them afterwards voids the claim from scoring and publishes a note saying so.
  • Two people are needed to resolve one. Whoever captured a claim does not get to decide whether it came true.
  • The verdict vocabulary contains no word for lying, hype or fraud. The Ledger says what was said, what happened, and whether they match — and then stops.
  • When the criteria turn out to be unscoreable, that is our failure, recorded on our scorecard, published with the same prominence as anything else.

What happens when this site is wrong

It goes on the corrections log, in public, with more room than the original claim got: what we said, what is true, and why we missed it. The log counts anything wrong that reached the published site — whoever caught it, including when it was us, and including when nobody would ever have noticed. Nothing is quietly edited away.

If you find an error, that is genuinely the most valuable thing you can send. A reader-found correction is published under your name.

What this site is not

  • Not financial advice, ever. Several pages discuss cryptography, Bitcoin and publicly traded companies. Nothing here is a recommendation to buy or sell anything, and no position is held in any company named on this site.
  • Not sponsored, and not affiliated. No vendor pays for coverage, reviews copy before it ships, or has any influence over a Ledger verdict. There are no ads.
  • Not a consensus view. Where the field genuinely disagrees, the pages say so and name the disagreement rather than picking a side and hiding the other one.
  • Not finished. Parts of this site are explicitly incomplete, and where that is true it says so on the page instead of pretending otherwise.

Telling us we got something wrong

The best route is the correction report — it goes straight to the desk and it is the one thing here that always gets answered.
