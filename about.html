About the desk

SymbiQ is written and kept by Chinmoy: one person, a stubborn test suite, and no money or ties to any company whose claims turn up on these pages.

The independence matters because most of what happens here is arguing with numbers. A figure you read last week turns out to be wrong, or right, or right about a different quantity than the headline implied. Anyone doing that from behind a curtain is asking for trust on credit. So instead of a biography, this page lays out the machinery, and every part of it can be checked from the outside.

How a number gets onto this site

Figures that carry weight come from the primary source: the paper, the standard, the regulation, the press release itself. A confident summary by somebody else doesn’t count. When a number is calculated rather than quoted, the calculation lives in open code that runs again before every release. Where you might reasonably want to see the working, the page shows it in a small receipt that opens beside the claim.

Three guards stand at the door, and any one of them can stop a release:

  • Arithmetic gets redone. Widget outputs, thresholds, game pars and resource estimates are recomputed by scripts, usually by trying every possible input instead of a hopeful handful. A game par that isn’t a proven optimum doesn’t ship.
  • Citations get checked for rot. Papers age like milk more often than wine. Every arXiv identifier on the site is looked up against arXiv itself: does the paper still exist, was it withdrawn, has it moved several versions past the figure we quote, and does our sentence name the right authors or title? The worst mistake this site has made was a stale number from a superseded paper, which is why this guard exists.
  • Sentences get checked against data. The nastiest bugs here were never sums. They were sentences that confidently contradicted a table sitting three inches below them, so the guards read prose now too.

Claims are judged by rules written before the answer is known

The Ledger tracks public, dated, falsifiable claims about quantum computing made by labs, executives and regulators. Its rules are the interesting part:

  • The resolution criteria, and the exact sources that will settle them, are frozen the day a claim is logged, long before its deadline. Moving the goalposts later removes the claim from scoring and leaves a public note explaining why.
  • Resolving a claim takes two people. Whoever logged it doesn’t get to mark it.
  • The verdict vocabulary has no word for lying, hype or fraud. The Ledger sets what was said beside what happened and leaves the adjectives to you.
  • If a claim turns out to be unscoreable, that is our failure. It goes on our scorecard, as visibly as anyone else’s miss.

What happens when this site is wrong

It goes on the corrections log, in public, with more room than the original mistake got: what we said, what is true, and how we missed it. The log counts anything wrong that reached the published site, whoever spotted it, including errors we caught ourselves and ones nobody else would have noticed. Nothing gets painted over.

An error you find is the most useful thing you can send us, and a reader-found correction runs under your name.

What this site is not

  • Not financial advice, ever. Several pages discuss cryptography, Bitcoin and listed companies. None of it is a recommendation to buy or sell, and no position is held in any company named on this site.
  • Not sponsored, and not affiliated. No vendor pays for coverage, sees copy before it runs, or gets a say in a Ledger verdict. There are no ads.
  • Not a consensus view. Where experts disagree, the page names the disagreement and gives both sides their best argument.
  • Not finished. Some sections are incomplete, and those sections say so on the page.

Telling us we got something wrong

The fastest route is the correction report. It lands on the desk directly, and it is the one message here that always gets a reply.
