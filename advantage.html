Advantage Watch

Every claim that a quantum device beat a classical computer, and what happened next. Last checked 2 October 2026.

How to read this

Each row is a published claim that a quantum device did something a classical computer could not, in the claimants' own words, and every classical response we could find in journals and on arXiv. Dates are the first public posting of each paper (the arXiv version where there is one, otherwise the journal date). A response counts toward 'days to a classical answer' only if it targets the same experiment and its authors say they ran it. The status is the desk's reading of those responses, not a verdict from either side.

Three statuses, and none of them is a ruling:

  • Matched. A published classical method reproduces or beats the experiment's reported benchmark, by its own authors' account.
  • Contested. Classical work claims to match the result at some scales, on some instances, or for a later experiment in the same line, and the dispute is not closed.
  • Standing. We found no published classical reproduction as of the check date. This is an absence in our search, not a proof.

The Ledger scores promises about the future on their deadlines. This page scores results that are already published, which are tested differently: by whether anyone could do the same thing without the quantum machine.

Each claim, with its sources

Random circuit sampling, 53 qubits, 20 cycles

Google Quantum AI · quantum supremacy claim · Status: Matched.

The claim (23 October 2019, Arute et al., Nature 574, 505): About 200 seconds to take a million samples, against an estimate of 10,000 years on a classical supercomputer.

What happened next

  • 21 October 2019, an estimate, not a run: arXiv:1910.09534. Pednault, Gunnels, Nannicini, Horesh and Wisnieff (IBM). Argued that such circuits can be simulated on the Summit supercomputer in a matter of days. An argument about cost, not a run.
  • 4 November 2021, a classical run: arXiv:2111.03011; Physical Review Letters 129, 090502 (2022). Pan, Chen and Zhang. One million uncorrelated bitstrings at a fidelity of about 0.0037 in about 15 hours on a cluster of 512 GPUs.
  • 27 June 2024, a classical run: arXiv:2406.18889; National Science Review 12, nwae317. Zhao, Zhong, Pan and co-authors. 1,432 GPUs; a higher linear cross-entropy score than the experiment and, by the authors' account, seven times faster than Sycamore's run.

Our reading. Two groups report running classical samplers that reach or exceed the experiment's score. The 10,000-year estimate did not survive; the experiment itself is not in doubt.

Gaussian boson sampling, up to 76 photon clicks (Jiuzhang)

USTC (Zhong, Pan and co-authors) · quantum computational advantage claim · Status: Contested.

The claim (3 December 2020, Zhong et al., Science 370, 1460): A sampling rate about 10^14 times faster than the best known classical simulation on supercomputers.

What happened next

  • 6 June 2023, a partial match: arXiv:2306.03709; Nature Physics 20, 1461 (2024). Oh, Liu, Alexeev, Fefferman and Jiang. A tensor-network sampler that, they report, simulates the ideal distribution better than the experiment does, for the largest Gaussian boson sampling experiment so far. The abstract does not name which experiment, so we do not count it as an answer to the 2020 one.

Our reading. A published classical sampler beats the largest experiment of its day on the authors' own measure, which weakens the whole line of experiments; whether it answers this one is not stated in the paper.

Kicked Ising dynamics on a 127-qubit processor, error-mitigated

IBM Quantum with UC Berkeley · 'utility' claim (IBM's own word; not called advantage) · Status: Matched.

The claim (14 June 2023, Kim et al., Nature 618, 500): Accurate expectation values from a noisy 127-qubit processor, beyond the reach of the brute-force classical methods of the day.

What happened next

  • 26 June 2023, a classical run: arXiv:2306.14887; PRX Quantum 5, 010308 (2024). Tindall, Fishman, Stoudenmire and Sels. Tensor networks with belief propagation, reported as significantly more accurate and precise than the experiment, and able to reach the thermodynamic limit.
  • 28 June 2023, a classical run: arXiv:2306.16372. Begusic and Chan. Fast classical simulation by sparse Pauli dynamics of the same experiment.

Our reading. Two independent classical methods simulated the experiment within two weeks of the paper. IBM did not call it advantage; it is on this list because it was widely read as one.

Random circuit sampling, 67 qubits, 32 cycles

Google Quantum AI · beyond-classical claim · Status: Standing.

The claim (October 2024, Morvan et al., Nature 634, 328): Beyond the capabilities of existing classical supercomputers at this size (Google's December 2024 Willow post put it at under five minutes against 10^25 years).

What happened next

  • 8 November 2022, a general result: arXiv:2211.03999; STOC 2023. Aharonov, Gao, Landau, Liu and Vazirani. A polynomial-time classical algorithm for noisy random circuit sampling at a constant noise rate per gate. Its authors say it is not practical in its present form and does not address finite-size experiments. It predates the claim, so it is context, not a response.

Our reading. We found no published classical reproduction of this experiment's samples. The one general result (above) is asymptotic and its authors say it does not cover experiments of this size. The 10^25-year figure is a model estimate and the 2019 estimate fell, so it is not a safe number.

Quantum annealing dynamics of spin glasses

D-Wave · quantum supremacy claim · Status: Contested.

The claim (1 March 2024, King et al., arXiv:2403.00910; Science 388, 199 (2025)): Annealing processors generate samples in close agreement with the Schrödinger equation faster than leading classical methods (tensor networks and neural networks).

What happened next

  • 7 March 2025, a classical run: arXiv:2503.05693. Tindall, Mello, Fishman, Stoudenmire and Sels (Flatiron Institute). Tensor networks with belief propagation, claimed to simulate the experiment's results efficiently on standard resources. A preprint at the time of posting.
  • 12 March 2025, a partial match: arXiv preprint, via The Quantum Insider's 13 March 2025 write-up. Mauron and Carleo (EPFL). A time-dependent variational Monte Carlo method matching or beating D-Wave's reported accuracy on systems up to 128 spins; not shown at D-Wave's largest instances.

Our reading. Two classical groups say they match the result, one on the experiment's own instances, one at smaller sizes. D-Wave's largest instances are not claimed by the second. Neither is settled in a journal.

This is also a claim on the Ledger, scored on its own deadline.

Out-of-time-order correlator ('Quantum Echoes') on Willow

Google Quantum AI · verifiable advantage claim · Status: Standing.

The claim (22 October 2025, Google Research post; Nature, 'Observation of constructive interference at the edge of quantum ergodicity'): About two hours on Willow against an estimate of 13,000 times longer on a classical supercomputer.

What happened next

  • 16 April 2026, an argument: arXiv:2604.15427. Bermejo, Villalonga, Ware, Vidal and Szasz. Argues that tensor networks with belief propagation cannot feasibly simulate the experiment. This supports the claim. One co-author, Benjamin Villalonga, is also an author of Google's random circuit sampling papers, so it is not an independent test.

Our reading. No published classical simulation found. Google's own post says its team spent an estimated ten person-years red-teaming the result with nine classical algorithms; that is the claimant's account.

Floquet dynamics on up to 74 qubits (IBM Heron with Qedma error suppression)

IBM and Qedma · quantum advantage claim · Status: Standing.

The claim (30 July 2026, IBM newsroom announcement): Quantum advantage on commercially available hardware; the circuits are released to the Quantum Advantage Tracker for classical benchmarking.

What happened next

No classical response recorded yet.

Our reading. Announced 64 days before this check. The circuits are public for classical attempts and we have not recorded a published reproduction. IBM's own 2025 prediction, that the wider community would confirm a case by the end of 2026, is tracked on the Ledger.

This is also a claim on the Ledger, scored on its own deadline.

What this page cannot tell you

  • A classical result is its authors’ claim. Most are preprints. We say whether a paper was published and which journal; we do not referee them.
  • “Standing” is an absence. It means we searched journals and arXiv on the check date and found nothing. A method nobody has published is not the same as a method nobody has.
  • Matching is not the same as being useless. A matched experiment can still have been a real, hard thing to build. The question here is only whether it did something classical computers could not.
  • Estimates fall. Every classical-cost figure in the claims above (“10,000 years”, “13,000 times longer”) is a claimant’s model, and the oldest one in the list has already been beaten by runs.

If a row is wrong, or a classical result is missing, tell us. See also what is still open and how we check.
