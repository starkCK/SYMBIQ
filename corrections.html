21 corrections, and not one of them was caught by a reader yet

Everything below was found by us — usually by a fresh reviewer paid to attack work that had already passed its own author. That is the system working, and it is also the system’s limit: a referee only catches what somebody thought to question. The reader slot is open, and it pays the most.

The pattern, stated because it keeps repeating: in six consecutive verification passes, almost nothing was a formula error. The failures were an input invented for a correct equation, a quantity labelled as its neighbour, a measurement generalised past what it measured, and a citation that was accurate on the day it was written and stale eighteen months later. We publish that pattern because knowing your own failure mode is worth more than any individual fix.

  1. 2026-08-06 Light mode, every page — style.css Code that broke, or silently did nothing
    What we said
    Nothing, out loud — which is the problem. The 🟢🟡🔴 tiers are this site’s central teaching device, and we shipped them for a year without ever checking them on a light background.
    What’s true
    The light-mode palette overrode the violet and the teal and left the other three alone, so against the light background green measured 1.67:1, yellow 1.46:1 and red 2.64:1. The minimum for body text is 4.5:1. Yellow at 1.46:1 is not “low contrast”; it is invisible. Every tier chip, every warning and every “look here” marker on the site was unreadable for any visitor whose device is set to light.
    Why we missed it
    The palette was fixed by hand, colour by colour, and three of them were simply never revisited. Nobody looked at the site in light mode, and no check measured it — a colour that is wrong still renders, so it never fails anything. The fix is measured, not eyeballed: the darkest versions that still read as the same colour, at 4.53–5.73:1 composited over their own tinted backgrounds. Dark mode is byte-identical.

    Caught by Our own audit, while measuring something else on the same page.

  2. 2026-08-06 Tap targets — the tier toggle, the corrections filter, every slider Code that broke, or silently did nothing
    What we said
    On 2026-08-03 we recorded that the 44-pixel touch-target debt was “fully paid” and that the last controls under the minimum had been fixed.
    What’s true
    It was paid, and then re-broken twice within three days by new work. The tier toggle and the corrections filter each carried an explicit min-height: 40px — deliberately set, four pixels short of our own rule. Every range slider on the site rendered at the browser default of 16px. Thirty-four card links, the Bloch gate buttons and seven contents-rail links also missed. Sixty-odd controls across nine pages.
    Why we missed it
    We measured the wrong thing, and then stopped measuring. A control clears the minimum for one of two reasons: because a rule says so, or because its label happens to wrap to two lines. Several of ours cleared it the second way, which made the rest look deliberate. The check now hit-tests the target rather than the box, at 375px, on all seventeen pages — that distinction matters, because a control given an invisible 44px band has a small box and a correct target.

    Caught by Our own audit, after a new page inherited the same defect.

  3. 2026-08-05 The Size Cliff — pqc.html An arithmetic or a stale figure
    What we said
    Nothing — but a great many published sources say an ML-DSA-65 signature is 3,293 bytes, and had we built the page the usual way we would have repeated it.
    What’s true
    3,309 bytes, which is what FIPS 204 specifies. 3,293 appears to be round-3 Dilithium3, the pre-standardisation parameter set, still circulating as though it were the final one.
    Why we missed it
    We did not, and that is the entry. The page signs a real message and measures the result rather than reading a table, so it cannot inherit a wrong constant — and within an hour of being built it disagreed with its own sources and turned out to be right. We log it because it is the clearest evidence we have for why the measuring is worth the effort.

    Caught by The widget itself, disagreeing with the literature.

  4. 2026-07-24 The Annealing Volcano — games.js, journey.html An arithmetic or a stale figure
    What we said
    Logarithmic cooling “needs about 22,000 steps — ninety times this game’s budget.”
    What’s true
    About 3,000 steps, which is twelve times the 240-step budget. (Reaching T = 0.2 really does take ~500 million.)
    Why we missed it
    Geman & Geman’s bound takes the deepest barrier in the landscape as its constant. We used c = 5. No landscape in the game has a barrier deeper than 4 — the Twin Calderas ridge. A correct formula with an invented input, which is this project’s signature failure. The honest numbers are more persuasive at low temperature, so the correction improved the argument.

    Caught by Our own self-referee pass, the day it shipped.

  5. 2026-07-30 The Feasible Region — topic 09, queueing The number was right, the words were wrong
    What we said
    A curve labelled “average wait”, reporting 2× / 5× / 20× at 50 / 80 / 95% utilisation — repeated in the axis, three point labels, the alt text, the caption and the FAQ schema.
    What’s true
    1/(1−ρ) is time in the system — queueing plus service. The average wait in the queue is ρ/(1−ρ) = 1, 4, 19. So the true waiting multipliers are 4× and 4.75×, not 2.5× and 4×.
    Why we missed it
    The figure contradicted its own mathematics box two paragraphs below it — and it understated the effect the whole topic exists to teach. The number was right; the noun attached to it was wrong.

    Caught by A fresh-session hostile referee.

  6. 2026-07-30 The Bottleneck — orgames.js The number was right, the words were wrong
    What we said
    “A player chasing the largest shadow price scores 31.5.”
    What’s true
    Greedy on the largest shadow price scores 34.5 — par. It wins. The strategy that actually scores 31.5 is greedy on price × step.
    Why we missed it
    Measured over all 243 purchase sequences in exact rational arithmetic, then reproduced by driving the shipped game. The arithmetic was right and bolted to the wrong strategy. The real lesson is sharper: the trap is not following the shadow price, it is forgetting to multiply it by how far it holds.

    Caught by A fresh-session hostile referee.

  7. 2026-07-30 The Feasible Region — topic 05, branch and bound An arithmetic or a stale figure
    What we said
    “A 60-item yes/no problem has more combinations than there are atoms in a human body.”
    What’s true
    260 = 1.15×1018. A human body holds roughly 7×1027 atoms — about ten orders of magnitude more. You would need a 93-item problem.
    Why we missed it
    An unchecked intuition pump, wrong in the direction that flattered our own argument. Replaced with a comparison that is true and checkable: 260 is about two and a half times the number of seconds since the Big Bang.

    Caught by A fresh-session hostile referee.

  8. 2026-07-30 The Prune — a figure on The Feasible Region An arithmetic or a stale figure
    What we said

    What’s true

    Why we missed it

  9. 2026-07-29 Every figure on the site — style.css Code that broke, or silently did nothing
    What we said
    Nothing, out loud. Figure labels were specified at 10–13.5 px after a “legibility fix”.
    What’s true
    font: 600 10px/1.2 inherit is an invalid shorthand, and a browser drops the whole declaration. Every figure label on the site had been rendering at 16 px for a week. The legibility fix never did anything at all.

  10. 2026-07-25 The Solver’s Path — journey.html Code that broke, or silently did nothing
    What we said
    Nothing. The page was dead on the live site for about a day.
    What’s true
    An unescaped apostrophe in the prose “this game’s whole budget”, inside a single-quoted JavaScript string, threw a SyntaxError. The 20 KB inline script never ran, so visitors got the cold open and no way into the game.
    Why we missed it
    It was introduced by a verification pass that checked all the arithmetic and never reloaded the page. A prose edit inside a code string is a code edit. Every script on the site is now parsed by CI on every push, and that check is self-tested — the build deliberately re-breaks this exact bug and fails if the guard accepts it.

    Caught by A routine link check. Now caught automatically.

  14. 2026-07-21 🟢 Basics — basics.html The number was right, the words were wrong
    What we said
    AlphaQubit makes about 30% fewer wrong calls than “the best hand-written rulebook.”
    What’s true
    30% is the margin against correlated matching — the fast, real-time class. Against the best and most careful decoder (tensor-network, far too slow to run live) the margin is about 6%.
    Why we missed it

  15. 2026-07-21 What is error correction? · The Race The number was right, the words were wrong
    What we said
    Willow’s Λ = 2.14 “per step, over d = 3 → 5 → 7.”
    What’s true
    Λ = 2.14 ± 0.02 is the error suppression from increasing the code distance by two, and the paper quotes it for the d = 5 → d = 7 step. (Nature 638, 920 (2025).)
    Why we missed it
    This is the most-repeated number on the site, and attribution — not arithmetic — is where this project fails. It has been re-checked against the paper on every pass since.

    Caught by Re-fetching the primary source.

  16. 2026-07-21 The Decoder Duel — qec.html The number was right, the words were wrong
    What we said
    The textbook decoder “cannot learn otherwise.”
    What’s true
    It can. Correlated matching expresses exactly this defect, and on this toy code every coupled pair lights a single check — an ordinary boundary edge. A device-calibrated matching decoder scores 87.8% against the naive 46.7%, which is about 95% of the available headroom.
    Why we missed it
    We wanted the game’s thesis to be stronger than it was. Reframed to “nobody told it about this chip” — which is both true and the actual AlphaQubit argument. The mathematics underneath the duel came back flawless; only the sentence was wrong.

  18. 2026-07-30 The Arcade — play.html The number was right, the words were wrong
    What we said
    “Every par is a proven minimum.”
    What’s true
    True of three games out of five. Circuit Golf, Grover’s Escape and Max-Cut have pars found by exhaustive search. The Annealing Volcano has no par — it has a pass mark set below the best schedule search could find, which is a threshold, not a proof. Quantum Tic-Tac-Toe has no par at all.
    Why we missed it
    A claim measured on three things and then written across five. The corrected sentence is more interesting, because it has to explain what the Volcano is scored on instead.

    Caught by A fresh-session hostile referee.

  19. 2026-07-14 Can quantum break Bitcoin? — bitcoin.html An arithmetic or a stale figure
    What we said
    Breaking one Bitcoin key needs “millions of error-corrected qubits”; the racing attack needs 1.9 billion; the gap from today’s hardware is about six orders of magnitude.
    What’s true
    A Google Quantum AI / Ethereum Foundation / Stanford estimate (arXiv:2603.28846, March 2026) puts it at under 1,200 logical qubits, under 90M Toffoli gates, and ≈500,000 physical qubits — roughly nine minutes from a primed state, because the first half of Shor’s algorithm can be precomputed from public curve parameters. The gap is about three orders of magnitude, not six.
    Why we missed it
    Every source on the page was transcribed correctly, and a newer primary source superseded all of them. Our own footnote had predicted this exact compounding — improvements arriving on the hardware and algorithm axes at once — and we never applied it to our own headline. Being correctly cited is not the same as being current.

    Caught by A fresh-session hostile referee, checking for staleness.

  23. 2026-07-14 Week 2 — Q14 (Grover crossover) An arithmetic or a stale figure
    What we said
    A grading rule: “accept ± a factor of 2 for the N/2 convention.”
    What’s true
    The factor of 2 sits inside √N, so it squares to a factor of 4 in N. The two conventions give 6.2×1011 and 2.5×1012. A student using the average-case convention lands 4× from the key — outside the band we published.
    Why we missed it
    The published answer was right and the published grading rule would have marked correct work wrong. Both now ship, with the convention stated.

Found something wrong? Tell us.

This is the single most valuable thing you can send us, and it carries the largest reward on the server — +150 Fidelity, more than any question, streak or raid pays. That is deliberate: a reader who checks our work is worth far more to us than a reader who trusts it.

You don't need to be certain, and you don't need to be polite about it. "This number looks off" is a perfectly good report. If you turn out to be right, the correction runs with your name on it.

Read by a human, always. If you'd rather do it in the open, post it in Discord — public catches are just as welcome, and easier for everyone else to learn from.

How we try to avoid needing this page

Every 🔴 post and Boss Question solution gets an independent hostile-referee verification pass before publishing. Facts from secondary coverage get spot-checked against primary sources (arXiv, company blogs). It is not a formality: those passes have returned hundreds of findings across the site, and the ones that reached a reader are the log above. The rest were caught before publication, which is the entire point of paying someone to attack your own work.
