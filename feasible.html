The Feasible Region

Everything that is actually possible. Nothing that isn't.
Operations research is the mathematics of deciding well when you can't have everything — and it is the field quantum computing is most often accused of being about to revolutionise.

The whole field in four lines

1. You have a decision to make, and you can't do everything — there are limits on time, money, people, trucks, machines, beds.
2. Write the limits down as inequalities. The set of choices that satisfy all of them at once is called the feasible region. Everything outside it is fantasy.
3. Write down what "good" means as a single number to push up or down. That's the objective.
4. Operations research is the body of mathematics that finds the best point in that region — and proves that nothing better exists.

That last clause is the part people miss, and it is the entire reason the field matters. A heuristic gives you an answer. OR gives you an answer plus a certificate that no better answer exists. ⟦Proven⟧

Why this deserves a page of its own

Almost every number in your day was set by one of these models. The route your parcel took. Which nurse is on shift tonight. What your flight cost, and why it changed twice while you were looking at it. Which warehouse your order shipped from. How the grid decided which power station to fire up at 6pm. This is not a niche academic discipline — it is the quiet infrastructure layer under logistics, energy, healthcare, manufacturing, and finance, and it has been running there since the 1940s.

It matters here for a second reason. When somebody tells you a quantum computer will "solve optimisation problems no classical computer can touch", this is the field they are making a claim about. You cannot evaluate that claim — you cannot even understand what is being asserted — without knowing what classical optimisation already does, how fast it already is, and where it genuinely struggles. Most quantum-optimisation hype survives entirely on the reader not knowing that a good MILP solver will chew through a model with hundreds of thousands of variables before lunch.

So the honest order of operations is: learn what the classical machinery does first. Then the quantum question becomes answerable instead of atmospheric. That is what this page and everything under it is for — and it is why it sits beside the quantum material rather than inside it.

The picture the whole field is named after

Three doors in

🟢 The shape of every one of these problems

Once you see the pattern you cannot unsee it. Every problem in this field has exactly three parts: the choices (what am I allowed to decide?), the limits (what stops me?), and the goal (what does better mean, as one number?). Nurse rostering, parcel routing, factory scheduling, portfolio construction and airline crew pairing are the same three questions wearing different clothes.

The skill that takes longest to learn is not the solving. Solvers are free and someone else wrote them. The skill is writing the problem down correctly — noticing that "we should keep customers happy" is not yet a number, that "a driver can't work 14 hours" is a constraint you forgot, and that the objective everyone agreed on in the meeting quietly rewards the wrong behaviour. Most failed optimisation projects failed here, not in the mathematics.

🟡 Linear programming, and the cliff behind it

If your objective and all your constraints are linear, you have a linear program, and you are in unreasonably good shape. The feasible region is a convex polytope, the optimum sits at a vertex, and the simplex method (Dantzig, 1947) walks vertex to vertex, improving every step. It is exponential in the worst case and almost never behaves that way in practice; interior-point methods (Karmarkar, 1984) give genuinely polynomial-time guarantees. Modern solvers handle models with hundreds of thousands of variables routinely. ⟦Proven⟧

Then you add one word — "integer" — and the ground gives way. Force the variables to be whole numbers and the problem becomes NP-hard (Karp, 1972). The convexity that made everything work is gone; the feasible set is now a scatter of isolated points, and, as the figure above shows, the answer can jump to a corner the continuous solution never pointed at. This is not a small technicality. It is the difference between "solved before you finish your coffee" and "might not finish this century", and it is why the entire apparatus of branch and bound, cutting planes and metaheuristics exists.

One more idea worth carrying away, because it is the most useful output in all of OR and almost nobody outside the field knows it exists: shadow prices. Solve the example above and you learn not only what to build but that one extra machine-hour is worth exactly 0.75 and one extra unit of material exactly 0.5. That tells you where to spend money next — which is usually the question the person paying for the model actually had. ⟦Proven⟧ — checked here against strong duality and confirmed by re-solving with each limit raised by one.

🔴 What is actually hard, stated precisely

Linear programming over the rationals is in P (Khachiyan, 1979, via the ellipsoid method; Karmarkar 1984 gave the first practical polynomial algorithm). Integer programming is NP-hard, and 0–1 integer programming was one of Karp's original 21 problems (1972). Strong duality gives every LP a dual whose optimum coincides exactly with the primal's — that coincidence is the certificate of optimality, and it is what lets a solver claim "proven optimal" rather than "best found".

Branch and bound (Land & Doig, 1960) exploits precisely this: solve the LP relaxation, and because its optimum is an upper bound on any integer solution below it, whole subtrees can be discarded without ever being searched. In the example above the relaxation gives 21 and the best known integer solution gives 20, so any branch whose relaxation falls below 20 is provably worthless — that discarded volume is where all the speed comes from. What makes a modern solver fast is not raw search but how aggressively it can prove regions empty.

The honest position on quantum: a quantum advantage on general combinatorial optimisation is ⟦Heuristic⟧, not proven, and the burden of proof sits with the claimant. Grover-style amplitude amplification gives a quadratic speedup on unstructured search ⟦Proven⟧ — but real optimisation problems are not unstructured, which is exactly what branch and bound exploits and what Grover cannot. No quantum algorithm is currently known to beat a good classical MILP solver on a problem anyone actually needs solved. That may change. It has not changed yet, and this page will say so on the day it does.

Two games where the mathematics does the judging

Neither of these can be won by feel. Both were built the way everything else here is: the answer was computed offline in exact arithmetic first, and the game only ever reports what that arithmetic says. Par is a proven number, not a designer's guess — so when you beat it, you beat it.

Game one · duality, made expensive

The Bottleneck

You have inherited a plant that is short of everything, and a board that will fund exactly one upgrade a quarter.

The old plant manager is certain it's machine time — the machines are always running, everyone can see it. Finance says material, because material is what shows up on the invoice. The union rep wants skilled hours. Three confident people, three different answers, and a meeting that goes nowhere.

There is a number that settles it. Every constraint in a linear programme has a shadow price: exactly what one more unit of that resource would add to your profit, derived from the same solve that told you what to build. It is not an estimate. It is the answer to "where should the next rupee go", and almost nobody outside this field knows it exists.

It also has a trap in it, and the trap is the game. A shadow price is a slope. It tells you what the next unit is worth — not the next ten. Relieve a bottleneck far enough and it stops being the bottleneck, at which point its price collapses to zero and your money is doing nothing. Buy the resource with the biggest number every quarter and you will finish three short of par. The ledger will tell you how far each price holds, if you think to ask.

Game two · how optimisation actually wins

The Prune

Four thousand and ninety-six possible hauls. You will look at about thirty, and prove the rest cannot win.

Here is the thing people get wrong about optimisation. They imagine a very fast computer checking every option. That is not what happens, and it could never be what happens — real problems have more combinations than there are atoms in anything. What actually happens is stranger and much better: the solver proves that entire regions cannot contain the answer, and never looks inside them.

The instrument is a bound. Before opening a branch you ask a deliberately easier question — what if I could take fractions of things? — and the answer to the easy question is provably at least as good as anything the hard question could give you. If even that optimistic figure can't beat a haul you already hold, everything below dies untouched. Not "probably worthless". Provably.

Two things will surprise you. The best opening move is not to explore the most promising branch — it's to grab any good haul quickly, because a strong answer in hand is what makes everything else prunable. And a cut near the top of the tree is worth exponentially more than a cut near the bottom, so the satisfying-looking work down at the leaves is nearly worthless. Play it breadth-first and you will open 1,388 branches. Played well, about thirty-five.

Where this touches quantum computing — three real bridges

These are not analogies. Each one is a place where the same mathematics is load-bearing on both sides, and you can go and play with all three right now.

QUBO and Ising ⟦Proven⟧ formulation

Nearly every combinatorial problem can be rewritten as minimising Σ wij si sj over spins of ±1 — the ground state of a magnet (Lucas, 2014). This is the format quantum annealers and QAOA actually consume, so it is the doorway every quantum optimisation claim has to walk through. Max-Cut is the cleanest example, and it is NP-hard.

Metaheuristics ⟦Heuristic⟧

Simulated annealing borrowed its cooling schedule from metallurgy and is the classical baseline that quantum annealing is imitating. Knowing how well the classical version does is the only way to judge whether the quantum one is beating anything. The proven cooling schedule is also, provably, uselessly slow.

Matching, inside the machine ⟦Proven⟧

The best one, and almost nobody says it out loud: a quantum computer's error correction runs in real time because of an operations research algorithm. Decoding the surface code is solved as minimum-weight perfect matching — Edmonds' blossom algorithm, 1965. OR is not adjacent to quantum computing here. It is a component inside it.

Start here, if you want the theory

Ideas worth meeting first, roughly in the order they build on each other. Each is genuinely beautiful, and none of them needs more than school algebra to understand even if the proofs get heavy.

Duality

Every optimisation problem has a shadow twin, and solving one solves the other. The twin of "maximise profit given these limits" is "what are these limits worth?" — and at the optimum, the two answers are the same number. It still feels like a trick after you have proved it.

Max-flow / min-cut

The most water you can push through a pipe network equals the capacity of the cheapest set of pipes you could cut to sever it. Two questions that sound unrelated turn out to be the same question (Ford & Fulkerson, 1956).

Matching

Pairing things up — workers to shifts, organs to patients, syndrome events to errors. Edmonds' 1965 paper on it is also where the modern definition of "efficient algorithm" comes from, which makes it one of the load-bearing papers of computer science.

Branch and bound

How to search a space too large to search: prove most of it cannot contain the answer, and never look there. The bound does the work, not the branching.

Dynamic programming

Bellman's principle — an optimal plan's tail is itself optimal. It is the backbone of shortest paths, inventory policy, and, when the world is uncertain, of reinforcement learning. This is the direct bridge from OR into modern AI.

Queueing theory

Why the queue explodes long before the servers are full. Utilisation past roughly 80% makes waiting time climb non-linearly — the reason hospitals, motorways and CPUs all fall over at the same shape of curve.

Questions people actually ask

Is operations research just applied maths, or a branch of computer science?

Historically it is neither — it began in wartime Britain in 1937 with a group of scientists asked to work out how to use radar well, and it kept the habit of starting from a real operation rather than from a theorem. Today it overlaps heavily with combinatorial optimisation in computer science and with parts of economics and statistics, and the boundaries are not worth policing.

Is OR the same thing as machine learning?

No, and the distinction is useful. Machine learning mostly predicts: given data, what will happen? OR mostly decides: given what will happen, what should we do about it, subject to what we cannot change? Real systems chain them — forecast demand with ML, then decide inventory with OR. The confusion is recent and mostly marketing.

Will quantum computers make this obsolete?

Not on any evidence currently available. Quantum optimisation is real research and worth watching, but there is no known quantum algorithm that beats a strong classical solver on a problem anyone actually needs solved, and the quadratic speedup that is proven for unstructured search does not apply to the structured problems where classical methods do their best work. Anyone telling you otherwise should be asked for the resource estimate.

Do I need to be good at maths to start?

To understand the ideas on this page, no — school algebra and a willingness to draw the picture is genuinely enough, which is why the picture is above. To write models professionally, you need linear algebra and comfort with proofs. To invent new algorithms, considerably more. The first step is much smaller than people expect.
