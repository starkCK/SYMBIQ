Operations research: the field that already decides your day

Every quantum-optimisation headline you have ever read is a claim about operations research — the mathematics of deciding well when you cannot have everything. It is not a footnote to quantum computing. It is a century-old discipline that is already running underneath your life, and you cannot judge a single quantum claim without it.

It routed your parcelAnd staffed tonight's hospital shift, and priced your flight twice while you looked at it.
It runs inside the quantum computerSurface-code decoding is minimum-weight matching — Edmonds, 1965. OR isn't adjacent to the machine. It's a part in it.
It is what the hype is about"Quantum will revolutionise optimisation" is a claim about this field. Learn it and the claim becomes checkable.
It is more learnable than it looksSchool algebra and one drawing gets you the core idea. The first step is much smaller than people expect.

So it gets its own front door here rather than being buried inside the quantum pages — a place to learn the theory for its own sake, and the reference point every quantum claim on this site is measured against.

Why the timing matters

This field does not move at the speed of press releases. Here is the single clearest example, and every number is from a primary paper: how many physical qubits it would take to steal one Bitcoin key inside the ten-minute window before your transaction confirms.

So what would you actually use one for?Seven applications, ranked by how much of each is evidence rather than expectation — molecules · breaking encryption · optimisation · machine learning · error correction · sensing

This is the question that gets answered worst. The honest position is that quantum computers are not general accelerators — they are good at a narrow and specific class of things, and the gap between that class and the marketing is where most of the confusion lives. Here is the real list, ranked by how much of it is evidence rather than expectation. Where a genuine operations-research problem sits underneath, there is a door to it.

Simulating molecules and materials the strongest case

Nature is quantum, so a quantum computer is the natural instrument for it. The standard example is nitrogenase: a bacterial enzyme that pulls nitrogen out of the air at ambient temperature and pressure, while the industrial process doing the same job needs enormous heat and pressure. Nobody can compute the enzyme's active site accurately, because the electron correlations are exactly what classical methods approximate away.

The same argument covers battery electrolytes, catalysts and superconductors. This is the one application where the machine is suited to the problem rather than borrowed for it — no data-loading toll, because the data is the physics.

Status: needs error-corrected machines that do not exist yet. Credible, not imminent. ⟦Frontier⟧ The near-term engine already runs — VQE →

Breaking — and replacing — today's encryption ⟦Proven⟧ algorithm

Shor's algorithm factors large numbers efficiently, which breaks RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography. The algorithm is proven; the machine is roughly three orders of magnitude beyond anything built. But the deadline is real anyway, because encrypted traffic captured today can be decrypted later — so the migration to post-quantum cryptography is happening now, on live systems, and that is a genuine engineering programme rather than a prediction.

Optimisation ⟦Heuristic⟧ — most claimed, least shown

Routing fleets, scheduling factories, building portfolios. This is the application you will see promised most often and demonstrated least — there is no known quantum algorithm that beats a strong classical solver on a problem anyone actually needs solved.

The reason is worth understanding rather than taking on trust. Grover's quadratic speedup is proven, but it applies to unstructured search, and real optimisation problems are dense with structure that classical methods exploit ruthlessly. A solver does not search the space; it proves most of it cannot contain the answer.

Machine learning mostly oversold

The headline speedups mostly die on the data-loading toll: to touch N classical numbers the machine must first load them, which costs about N operations and eats the advantage before the algorithm starts. Several flagship claims were later matched by classical algorithms outright.

The surviving exception is the same as the first card: learning from data that was born quantum. Four routes, ranked honestly →

Error correction, right now ⟦Proven⟧ · shipping

Not an application of quantum computing so much as the thing standing between us and all of the above — and the one place where the loop this site is named after is already closed. AI decoders read the error patterns; a 1965 graph-matching algorithm does the repair in under a microsecond.

◆ Operations research, running inside the machine. Topic 07: Matching ▸

Quantum sensing ⟦Proven⟧ · deployed

Worth naming because it is the quantum technology that already works and nobody argues about. Atomic clocks, magnetometers and gravimeters exploit the same physics without needing a computer at all — used today in navigation, medical imaging and surveying. It is not on this list to pad it out; it is here because "quantum" delivering real value today mostly means sensing, not computing, and conflating the two is a common way to be misled.

How this place actually runsWhat is running today · what was designed and has not started · why the desk edits rather than crowdsources · and why a correction runs louder than the mistake

What runs today: the pages, the games, and The Question — every question numbered and dated, so you see the real cadence rather than a promised one. That cadence is currently slow, and the dates say so: seven questions between 19 July and 4 August 2026, none since. The Ledger also runs today: every dated, public, falsifiable claim about quantum computing — from labs, executives and regulators — logged the day it is made and resolved only when its deadline arrives, with our own error rate published next to theirs. Nothing has resolved yet, which is the honest starting state.

What was designed and has not started: a nightly Collapse naming the day’s best answer, a weekend Boss Question the room fights together, and The Lattice — a shared code distance that grows with whatever the community earns. None of those have ever run. They need a room with people in it, and the Discord is still close to empty. We would rather say that here than advertise a ritual you would turn up for and not find. The Frontier is the newest of these: curated open questions, each meant to run next to a panel of frontier AI models asked the same thing verbatim — the questions are written, the panel has not been run yet.

What is true regardless of how busy the room gets: SymbiQ is edited, not crowdsourced — anything a reader sends is reviewed by the desk before it goes anywhere, and if it ships, it ships with your name on it. Catching an error in our content is the contribution we prize above all others, and when we get something wrong the correction runs with more room than the mistake (Corrections). The Race is meant to be the scoreboard for the six labs chasing fault tolerance, with our own predictions scored in public — it is still empty, because inventing a lab status to fill a table is exactly what that page exists to argue against.
