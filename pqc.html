What does post-quantum cryptography cost on the wire?

Not in theory. In bytes, measured in this browser, from keys generated a moment ago.

Three tools: The Size Cliffwhat PQ costs on the wire The Inventoryread your own artefacts The Sequencerwhen you can actually finish

The answer, in four lines

Post-quantum TLS is not slow because the mathematics is slow. ML-KEM is fast — thousands of key generations a second in JavaScript. It is expensive because the objects are large, and the network was built around the assumption that they are small.

The key exchange is the famous problem and the smaller one. A hybrid key share pushes the ClientHello past one packet. That is a one-time cost, and browsers already work around it.

The signature is the real problem. A certificate chain carries several signatures, and a post-quantum signature is roughly fifty times larger than the one it replaces. Past a threshold the server's reply no longer fits the initial congestion window, and every new connection pays an extra round trip.

And the obvious metric misleads. Pick a signature scheme by public-key size and you will choose the one with an eight-kilobyte signature. Move the controls below and watch which choice actually breaks.

The Size Cliff

Every cryptographic number below is the length of a real object — a key, a ciphertext, or a signature — that your browser generated when you clicked. Nothing is read from a table. That is the point of building it this way: a lookup table inherits whatever error was in its source, and this one caught a live example. Several published sources state that an ML-DSA-65 signature is 3,293 bytes. Sign something and measure it and the answer is 3,309, which is what FIPS 204 specifies. The widget cannot make that mistake, because it never looks anything up.

This figure needs JavaScript — it generates real post-quantum keys in your browser. Nothing is sent anywhere.

What is measured, and what is modelled

The distinction is stated on every row rather than averaged away, because mixing the two is how confident wrong numbers get published.

  • Measured — key shares, ciphertexts, public keys and signatures. Exact, from real ML-KEM, ML-DSA and SLH-DSA operations, plus X25519 and ECDSA from your browser's own WebCrypto.
  • Modelled — TLS record and handshake framing, and the non-key parts of an X.509 certificate. Real handshakes vary with client, server and extension set.

So the absolute totals are a model; the differences between two choices are exact. The framing is identical on both sides of any comparison, so it cancels — and the difference is the whole subject. When the widget says a hybrid key share costs 1,184 bytes more than X25519, that number is measured, not assumed.

The limits it is measured against are real: a 1,460-byte TCP payload is what fits in one packet on a standard 1,500-byte Ethernet MTU, and the initial congestion window has defaulted to ten segments since Linux 2.6.39 (RFC 6928). A server flight past 14,600 bytes waits for an acknowledgement before it can finish.

The trap worth failing once

Set the signature to SLH-DSA-128s. Its public key is 32 bytes — smaller than ML-DSA-65's 1,952, and exactly the same size as Ed25519's. By the metric most people reach for first, it is the compact choice.

Its signature is 7,856 bytes. A chain carries more signatures than public keys, so the scheme with the smallest key produces by far the largest handshake. SLH-DSA earns that cost honestly — it rests only on the security of a hash function, with no lattice assumption to be wrong about — but the trade is the opposite of the one the key size advertises.

The Inventory

The tool below this one plans a migration across whatever estate you describe. Its own honest-limits list says the obvious thing about that: it discovers nothing. A migration programme lives or dies on the inventory, and the inventory is the hard part.

So start here instead. Paste the artefacts you already have — a certificate chain, the contents of an authorized_keys file, a JWKS document — and this reads them. Not pattern-matches them: reads them, decoding the ASN.1 byte by byte, the same structure openssl x509 walks. Every field in the table is taken out of the artefact. Nothing is entered by you, and nothing is looked up in a table of what a key of that name is supposed to be.

And the parse is checked against a second implementation. For every key your browser's own WebCrypto can import, we hand it the exact bytes and read the algorithm back from the browser rather than from us. Agreement earns the row a CONFIRMED tag. Where WebCrypto has no algorithm at all — ML-DSA and SLH-DSA today, Ed25519 on older browsers — the row says PARSED, which means our decoder alone read it and nothing independent has checked it. The tag names which quantity was checked, not how confident we feel.

This tool parses in your browser. It needs JavaScript. Nothing you paste is transmitted — there is no server to transmit it to.

The finding that is worth the whole tool

A certificate contains two algorithms, not one. There is the key it carries, and there is the algorithm its issuer used to sign it. They are usually different, and they usually belong to different people. An inventory with one algorithm column per certificate silently loses half the estate — and the half it loses is the half you may not control.

Load the example and look at api.example.com: an ECDSA P-256 key, signed sha256WithRSAEncryption. Rotating that leaf to ML-DSA changes nothing about the RSA signature above it. If that issuer is your own internal CA, it is a prerequisite and the sequencer below will schedule it as one. If it is a public CA, it is not an engineering task at all — it is a procurement conversation, on somebody else's timetable.

And the one it will not let you miss

Your harvest-now-decrypt-later exposure is not in any of these files. In TLS 1.3 the certificate key signs; it never encrypts. The key exchange that actually protects the traffic is an ephemeral ECDHE or X25519 share negotiated per connection, and it appears in no certificate, no authorized_keys file and no certificate-derived CBOM.

That distinction decides which risks are retroactive. A signature algorithm broken in 2032 cannot forge a 2026 handshake after the fact — forgery only works forwards. A key exchange broken in 2032 decrypts every recording anybody made of it in 2026. So the asset with the worst exposure in most estates is the one no file will hand you, and the tool tells you to add it by hand rather than quietly scoring you on what it happened to be able to read.

What comes out

  • A CycloneDX 1.6 CBOM. The field names and enumerated values come from the published 1.6 JSON schema, and each certificate is emitted as three linked components — the certificate, the algorithm of the key it carries, and the algorithm it was signed with — because that is the split the format was built to express. Validate it with cyclonedx-cli before you file it anywhere that matters; we cannot run the official validator inside a page with no network.
  • The dependency edges, read off the chain. Any certificate whose issuer is also in your paste becomes dependent on it. That is the input the sequencer below needs and the input nobody enjoys typing.
  • A confidentiality lifetime of zero on every signing key, with the reason stated rather than assumed. Padding an inventory is the commonest way one of these programmes ends up looking bigger than it is, and a signing key that cannot be forged backwards has no confidentiality lifetime to overstate.

Your estate, not ours

The figure above measures a decision. This one measures you. Describe what you actually run — the rows below start from a realistic example, and every one of them is editable — and it will tell you which of your assets are already past saving, in what order to migrate, and whether your deadline is reachable at all.

The idea that makes this different from a readiness scorecard. Mosca's inequality says you are exposed when X (how long the data must stay secret) plus Y (how long migration takes) exceeds Z (how long until a quantum computer can break it). Almost everyone plugs in an asset's own effort as Y. That is wrong the moment anything depends on anything. Your real Y is when the plan actually reaches that asset — an embedded fleet sitting behind a code-signing pipeline sitting behind a certificate authority does not take eight quarters, it takes however long all three take in sequence at your actual capacity. So the schedule feeds back into the risk, and changing the order changes who is exposed. Change it below and watch the last column move.

And nobody knows Z. Predicting it is what every other tool in this space does and none of them can justify it. So the last column does not predict: it reports the breakeven — this asset is exposed unless a cryptographically relevant quantum computer is further away than N years. That is a claim you can check against your own belief instead of adopting ours.

How to read the result

  • Two different infeasibilities, and they need opposite responses. If you are short of total capacity, no ordering fixes it and the tool tells you the minimum team size. If instead the dependency chain is longer than the time remaining, then you have enough people and it still cannot be done — work that must happen in sequence cannot be parallelised by hiring. Conflating those two is how migration programmes get funded and still miss.
  • Ordering is worth real years. The comparison line under the table runs all four policies against your estate at identical capacity and constraints. On the example estate the spread is about three-quarters of a year of worst-case exposure — for free, by changing nothing but sequence.
  • AES-256 is not migration work and the tool refuses to count it. Grover halves symmetric strength, which AES-256 absorbs. Padding an inventory with symmetric crypto is the most common way these programmes look bigger than they are.
  • A vendor asset is marked, not solved. If you do not control it, your migration date is theirs. The tool schedules it because your exposure is real either way — but the lever is procurement, not engineering.

Honest limits on this one

  • It still discovers nothing by itself. You type your estate in, and it is exactly as good as what you type — unless you fill it from the Inventory above, which reads real artefacts and hands the rows down with the dependency edges already in place. Even then the effort column is yours: nothing in a certificate knows how long your migration takes.
  • Effort in quarters is a planning fiction, the same one every roadmap uses. It is useful for comparing orderings against each other and poor for predicting a calendar date.
  • One dependency per asset in this version. Real estates have several; the engine handles the general case, the editor does not yet.
  • Nothing leaves your browser. Export writes a file locally. There is no analytics on what you type, because there is no request.

Why this has a date on it

This stopped being a research question. Each of these is a published instrument with a number in it:

  • Executive Order 14412 (signed 22 June 2026) — federal high-value assets on post-quantum key establishment by 31 December 2030 and post-quantum signatures by 31 December 2031. It also orders CISA and NIST to publish the minimum elements of a cryptographic bill of materials within 270 days — about 20 March 2027.
  • NIST IR 8547 — RSA and elliptic-curve public-key cryptography deprecated after 2030, disallowed after 2035.
  • CNSA 2.0 — quantum-resistant algorithms required for new national security system acquisitions from 2027, exclusive use by 2033.
  • The EU coordinated roadmap — member states to hold cryptographic inventories by end of 2026, high-risk systems migrated by 2030.

And the reason none of those dates can simply be waited out: harvest now, decrypt later. Traffic captured today is decrypted whenever the capability arrives, so the exposure is retroactive. Michele Mosca's inequality states it in one line — if X (how long your data must stay secret) plus Y (how long your migration takes) exceeds Z (how long until a cryptographically relevant quantum computer), you are already late. ⟦Proven⟧ the arithmetic; ⟦Frontier⟧ the value of Z Nobody knows Z. That is exactly why X and Y are the only two you control.

What a machine capable of this would actually have to be is a separate question, answered with resource estimates rather than adjectives, on the Bitcoin page.

Honest limits

Stated plainly, because a page about cryptography that oversells itself is worse than useless.

  • This is an instrument, not a range. It measures byte costs. It does not touch your infrastructure, discover your certificates, or rehearse a migration. If you want a hands-on post-quantum PKI to play with, Keyfactor's PQC Lab, Codegic and Utimaco's simulator all exist and are free. They demonstrate products; this measures a decision.
  • The cryptographic library here is not independently audited and has no side-channel protection. It is @noble/post-quantum, MIT-licensed, vendored into this site so no third party is contacted when you load the page. It must never be used to protect a real secret, and it never handles one here — every key it generates is discarded on the next click. We verified it the only way that means anything: 28 checks against the byte lengths fixed by FIPS 203, 204 and 205, plus encapsulate/decapsulate agreement and signature accept-and-reject.
  • Nothing you do on this page leaves your browser. There is no server. Key generation, measurement and rendering all happen locally.
  • The framing model is ours, and a real deployment will differ. The numbers to trust here are the differences, not the totals.
