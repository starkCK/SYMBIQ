The Race

The scoreboard on the labs racing to fault tolerance — and our own prediction record, kept publicly. Both tables are still empty, and saying so is the point. We keep score on ourselves so you can decide whether to trust us.

What the race is actually scored on

Qubit counts make the headlines. They are close to meaningless on their own. The number that decides whether a machine is in this race at all is Λ — how much the logical error rate shrinks each time you make the code bigger.

The rule, from the scaling law on the QEC page: raising the code distance by two (d → d+2 — one step, and that is what the word means everywhere below) divides the logical error rate by Λ. Raising an odd d by one buys nothing at all. Below threshold Λ > 1 and errors fall away geometrically as you add qubits. Above threshold Λ < 1 and the same extra qubits make the machine worse. Same hardware, same code, opposite direction — and the whole industry is sorted by which side of that line it sits on.

Drag the line

Logical error per round, relative to d=3 — shorter is better. Logarithmic scale: the axis runs from 10⁻⁴ (floor) to 10² (full height), so a bar that is one sixth of the axis shorter than its neighbour is ten times better, and one that is half the axis shorter is a thousand times better. Read the number on the bar, not the picture.

Honest model: this is the standard below-threshold scaling form pL ∝ (p/pth)⌊(d+1)/2⌋, plotted as a ratio to d=3 — which is why no fitted prefactor appears and none is needed. It is the shape of the curve, not a prediction of any specific chip's error rate. Real devices depart from it near threshold, and Λ itself drifts with calibration. One more honest note on Λ: here it is set by the slider as the suppression-per-two-steps directly, and the scaling form above is what ties that to the ratio p/pth — in a real device the two agree only as well as the model does. Willow's Λ = 2.14 ± 0.02 is a measured suppression factor for increasing the code distance by two, quoted for the d = 5 → 7 step of a d = 3, 5, 7 series (arXiv 2408.13687, Nature 638, 920 (2025)).
